Everything has a number

Every room, door, person and object in your world has a number: #1, #200, #4051. You will use those numbers constantly, because they are how you point at something precisely — there may be four swords in the room, but there is only one #4051.

To find out what something is, stand near it and ask:

> +show sword # quick summary, including its number > @examine #4051 # everything: what it is, what it can do

Things are also built from other things. A chair is made from the furniture prototype, a bottle from the container one. Whatever you build from, your new object starts out able to do everything that one could — so a chair you make can be sat on without you arranging anything.

Rooms and doors

Dig a room, then connect it. Digging creates the room off to one side; it is the exits that put it on the map.

> @dig ICRoom = The Old Library Created room #412. > @open north to #412 # a compass exit from where you stand

Exits come in kinds, and the kind decides how players deal with it:

To makeUse
A compass direction@open north to #412
Something you walk through — a gate, an arch@gopen gate to #412
A door that opens, closes and locks@dopen oak door to #412
Stairs or a ladder, to be climbed@copen stairs to #412
A gap, to be jumped@jopen chasm to #412

Each of these makes the return exit as well. Add /noret for a one-way trip.

Things

@make takes what to build from, and what to call it:

> @make item = sword > @desc sword = A plain iron blade, notched near the hilt.

Useful starting points are item (anything carriable), object (scenery that stays put), BaseContainer (things that hold other things), BaseFurniture and BaseWearable. @examine on something that already behaves the way you want will tell you what it was built from, which is usually the fastest way to find the right one.

Shared behaviour goes on the parent

If every lantern in your world should do something, put it on a lantern prototype and build the lanterns from that. If exactly one lantern is strange, put it on that lantern — see making one object behave differently.

Naming, and why it matters

You do not type a display name in directly. You give an object a noun, and optionally an article, up to three adjectives, and a trailer — and the game assembles the name from those parts.

> @name #4051 = sword > @adjective #4051 = old rusty "an old rusty sword"

It sorts out a and an for you as the adjectives change. The reason for the fuss is that this is also how players refer to things. Because rusty is kept as a fact about the sword rather than buried in a line of text, all of these find it — and get 2nd sword picks the second one in the room:

> get sword > get rusty sword > get the old rusty sword
Keep contents out of names

A case of whiskey should be called "a case", not "a case of fire whiskey". Names are what players type at, so putting the contents in the name means drink whiskey starts matching the box instead. Say what is inside in the description, or let them look in it.
